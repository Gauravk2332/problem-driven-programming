Six coins are used. For this specific total, any use of fewer than two 25s leaves at least 38 for at most four smaller coins; with exactly one 25 and four smaller coins, 38 cannot be formed because the only way to get near 38 is 4 tens = 40. With two 25s, the remaining 13 needs at least four coins. Hence six is optimal here (this is not a general proof for arbitrary denominations).

Chapter 02 / 07

Understand the greedy rule

A greedy algorithm builds a feasible solution by repeatedly making a locally preferred choice and committing to it. The choice must be justified for the specific problem; it need not be the largest number.

Greedy-choice property: some globally optimal solution contains the proposed first greedy choice. Optimal substructure: after that choice, the remaining problem can be solved optimally and combined with it.

Chapter 03 / 07

Work through the choices

Use coins {1,3,4}, total 6. Greedy chooses 4,1,1; optimum uses 3,3. The remaining amount is a valid smaller problem, but the first greedy choice is unsafe. Optimal substructure alone does not imply greedy correctness.

MethodCoinsCount
Greedy4,1,13
Optimal3,32

Exchange proof: start with an optimum, show you can replace its first choice by the greedy choice without making it worse, then repeat on the residual problem. A counterexample disproves a universal claim; examples that pass cannot prove it.

def greedy_change(coins, amount):
    used=[]
    for c in sorted(coins, reverse=True):
        q,amount=divmod(amount,c)
        used.extend([c]*q)
    return used if amount==0 else None
print(greedy_change([1,3,4],6))
